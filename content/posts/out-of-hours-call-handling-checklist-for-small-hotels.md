A small hotel handles out-of-hours calls well when it has decided five things in advance: when "out of hours" starts and ends, who answers, how an urgent call reaches a person, what every other caller is told, and who follows up in the morning. This checklist walks through each one so you can fill it in for your own property in about half an hour.

> **Disclosure.** We make Hanas, an AI receptionist for small hotels, so we have a stake in how this topic is handled. The checklist works for any set-up: voicemail, an answering service, a night porter, a mobile on the bedside table or an AI receptionist. This is general guidance, not legal advice.**Key Takeaways**Decide the hours first. "Out of hours" is whenever nobody at the property is free to take a call, which often includes busy check-in and breakfast periods, not just nights.Separate urgent calls (a guest in the building needs help) from everything else. Only the first needs to wake someone.Tell callers what will happen next, in one sentence, and then do it. Silence after a message is what loses the booking.Assign the morning follow-up to a named person with a time. A taken message nobody reads is the same as a missed call.

## Step 1: Define what "out of hours" means for you

Write down the real times, not the times on the website. Many small hotels find three different windows:

- **Overnight**, after the last staff member leaves or goes off duty.
- **Busy periods**, such as check-in, check-out and breakfast, when the person on the desk is dealing with guests in front of them.
- **Days off and quiet shifts**, when one person covers everything.

Each window may need a different answer. Overnight might go to an on-call contact for emergencies only; a busy breakfast might only need a quick "we will call you back within the hour". Our post on [how many calls a small hotel misses](https://gethanas.com/blog/how-many-calls-does-a-small-hotel-miss-what-it-costs) explains how to count your own.

## Step 2: Decide who or what answers

There are five common options. None is right for every property.

1. **Voicemail.** Cheapest. Callers must be willing to leave a message and wait.
2. **A night porter or live-in owner.** A person who can help in the building. Costs and rota limits apply.
3. **A human answering service.** A person who is not at the hotel takes messages from a script you give them.
4. **A call-forward to a mobile.** Fast, but it depends on someone being awake, in signal and willing.
5. **An AI receptionist.** Answers every call and takes details, within the limits its vendor sets.

For a side-by-side look at three of these, see our post comparing an [AI voice agent, an answering service and a night porter](https://gethanas.com/blog/ai-voice-agent-vs-answering-service-vs-night-porter-which-fits-a-small-uk-hotel).

## Step 3: Sort calls into types

Not every call deserves the same response. A simple split is enough to start:

- **Urgent, in-house guest.** A locked-out guest, a safety worry, a problem with the room. This needs a person, now.
- **New booking enquiry.** Dates, room type and number of guests. Important, but it can usually wait until the morning if the caller is told when to expect a reply.
- **Change to an existing booking.** Log it clearly for staff to action.
- **Everything else.** Directions, parking, breakfast times: answer from your published information, or take a note.

Write down which of these genuinely justify waking someone, and give that person a clear list. If your emergency procedure covers fire, medical or security incidents, it should already exist separately; this checklist does not replace it.

## Step 4: Script what the caller hears

Whoever answers, the caller should leave the call knowing three things: that they reached the right place, what has been noted, and what happens next. A short script covers it:

> "Thank you for calling [hotel]. The front desk is closed until [time]. If you are a guest in the hotel and need urgent help, [do this]. Otherwise I can take your details and the team will reply by [time]."

Keep it honest. Do not promise a room, a rate or a confirmation unless the person or system answering is actually able to give one. Our post on [booking requests versus confirmed bookings](https://gethanas.com/blog/booking-request-vs-confirmed-booking-what-is-the-difference) covers the wording.

If an AI answers, it should say it is an AI at the start of the call. If calls are recorded, the opening should say so too; see our guide to the [recording notice at the start of a call](https://gethanas.com/blog/call-recording-notice-for-hotels-what-to-say-at-the-start-of-a-call).

## Step 5: Capture the right details

For a booking enquiry, take the same fields every time so staff do not have to call back to ask:

- Name and best phone number (and email if offered).
- Arrival and departure dates, number of guests and room type.
- Language, if it is not English.
- Special requests: accessibility, dogs, late arrival, dietary needs.
- Anything the caller asked that nobody could answer.

Take only what you need. Personal details collected on calls are covered by UK GDPR, so decide how long you keep them and who can see them.

## Step 6: Own the morning follow-up

This is the step most small hotels skip. Decide:

- **Who** checks the messages, requests or dashboard first thing.
- **By when** each type of call gets a reply (for example, booking enquiries before 10:00). Pick a time you can meet.
- **How** staff reply: phone, email or both, in the caller's language where you can.
- **What happens** to a request nobody answers, and who is alerted.

If you use an AI receptionist that sends requests for staff approval, the follow-up step is where the booking is won or lost. According to its own site (read 21 November 2026), Hanas sends a request by email with an approve or decline button and keeps it in a dashboard; the site says the AI does not confirm a booking by default. We describe that as the current default, not a permanent rule.

## The checklist in one place

1. Out-of-hours windows written down (overnight, busy periods, days off).
2. Chosen answerer for each window.
3. Definition of "urgent" and a named on-call contact.
4. One short script for callers, including who they are speaking to.
5. Fixed list of details to capture.
6. Named person and deadline for the morning follow-up.
7. A way to hear or read a sample of calls each week.
8. Review every quarter or when staffing changes.

## Frequently asked questions

### What counts as out of hours for a small hotel?

Any time nobody can take a call properly. That usually means overnight, and often busy check-in and breakfast periods too.

### Should every out-of-hours call wake someone?

No. Keep that for urgent calls from guests in the building. Booking enquiries and general questions can usually wait for the morning if the caller is told what happens next.

### Is voicemail enough?

For some hotels it is. The trade-off is that callers who want a room now may ring another hotel instead. We have no measured figure for how often, so we will not invent one.

### Does an AI receptionist replace the night porter?

Not if guests in the building need help in person. An AI can take a message or route an urgent call as set up, but it cannot walk down a corridor. The two can work together.

### How often should I review the process?

Listen to or read a few calls each week at first, then quarterly, or whenever opening hours or staffing change.
