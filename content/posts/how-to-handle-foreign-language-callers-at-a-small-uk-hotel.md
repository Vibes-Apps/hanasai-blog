## How should a small UK hotel handle foreign-language callers?

Plan for the calls before they arrive. Know which languages your guests are most likely to use, keep short scripts that confirm details in writing, and give callers a second route (email or a booking form) for anything where a mistake is costly: names, dates, dietary needs and access needs. No single tool removes the risk, because speech and translation tools can still mishear names and numbers. The most reliable habit is a read-back, and a written confirmation after the call.

## What goes wrong on a call with a language barrier?

The general request is usually understood. The trouble is in small details that are easy to mishear and expensive to get wrong.

![Five tiles showing the details that go wrong on calls with a language barrier: names, dates, dietary needs, access needs, directions](/blog/images/foreign-language-callers-small-hotel-language-barrier-failure-points.png)

_The five details worth confirming in writing after any call with a language barrier._

- **Names and spelling.** A surname from another alphabet, or one with unfamiliar letter patterns, is easy to mis-spell. A wrong name on a booking can cause trouble at check-in or with a payment card.
- **Dates and numbers.** Date order differs between countries, and phone numbers and group sizes can be misheard. A booking for the wrong night is an expensive mistake.
- **Dietary needs.** "No nuts", "vegetarian" and "halal" are simple words, but allergies carry real risk. A vague answer should not be treated as confirmed.
- **Accessibility needs.** A caller may struggle to describe a mobility need or a hearing or vision need in a second language, and staff may not ask the right follow-up question.
- **Directions and arrival.** Place names, car parks, key collection and late arrivals are easy to garble over the phone.

## What are your practical options?

Most small hotels combine several. These are general trade-offs, not tested results.

| Option | Strength | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Staff who speak the language | Natural, fast | Covers only the languages and hours they work |
| Translation app on a phone or tablet | Cheap, many languages | Awkward mid-call; names, numbers and accents can be mistranslated |
| Scripts and phrase cards | Free, predictable | Covers only the situations you wrote |
| Email or form fallback | Written record, time to translate | Slower; the caller must switch |
| Answering service with language support | Interpreters or multilingual agents | Cost, languages and hours vary by provider |
| AI receptionist with multilingual support | Answers at any hour, several languages | Quality differs by language; names and numbers can still be misheard |

![Flow diagram: a foreign-language call arrives, the caller is greeted, details are read back, a written confirmation is sent by email, and unresolved items go to a staff member](/blog/images/foreign-language-callers-small-hotel-foreign-language-call-fallback-flow.png)

_A simple fallback for a call you cannot fully understand: greet, read back, confirm in writing, escalate._

**Staff languages and answering services.** A team member who speaks the language is the best option, but calls arrive at all hours. Some answering services offer multilingual agents or interpreters. We reviewed none, so we make no claims about their languages, hours or prices. Ask which languages are covered, when, how details reach you, and how recordings are handled.

**Translation apps.** They help for a short exchange. For names, numbers and anything safety-related, treat the output as a draft. We cite no accuracy figure because we read no source that gives one. This is a general caution: machine translation and speech recognition can fail on names, numbers and accented speech even when the rest of the conversation goes well.

**Scripts, phrase cards and a written fallback.** A card by the phone with short phrases in each of your top languages saves time ("Please spell your surname", "Please give the arrival date", "Do you have any food allergies?", "Do you need step-free access?"). Then ask for an email address and send a short confirmation: the name as you have written it, dates, room, dietary and access notes. Callers can often correct a written error more easily than a spoken one. A short booking-request form on your website gives them a way to avoid the phone altogether.

**AI receptionists.** A multilingual AI receptionist answers when no one can. The risks are those of any speech tool, so ask what it does when unsure.

## Is it fair to every caller? Accessibility and equality

Language is not itself listed as a protected characteristic, but the GOV.UK page on [types of discrimination](https://www.gov.uk/discrimination-your-rights/types-of-discrimination) lists "race including colour, nationality, ethnic or national origin" and "disability" among them, and says protection applies to people as consumers. This is general information, not legal advice. If you are unsure how it applies to your hotel, ask a qualified adviser or read our companion piece, [Is it legal to use an AI receptionist at a UK hotel?](https://gethanas.com/blog/is-it-legal-to-use-an-ai-receptionist-at-a-uk-hotel).

Two practical points follow. First, use one consistent fallback (email, written confirmation) for every caller. Second, plan for disabled customers in the same system. GOV.UK's [quick start guide for service providers](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/equality-act-guidance/disability-quick-start-guide-for-service-providers-html) (updated 8 March 2013) says there is "a legal requirement to make reasonable changes to the way things are done", gives "provide information in an accessible format" as an example, and says providers should not wait until a disabled person experiences difficulties. A phone-only route may be hard for a deaf or hard-of-hearing caller, so an email or form route helps them as well as language-barrier callers. Equality Act duties are the hotel's to meet; no phone tool meets them for you.
