If a UK hotel records calls, the safest habit is to tell callers at the start of every call, in one short sentence, that the call is recorded. A line such as "Calls are recorded for training and quality" is typical. The legislation we read asks businesses to make all reasonable efforts to inform people that calls may be intercepted, and the ICO's guidance says customers should be informed during calls and pointed to fuller privacy information. This article explains both, gives example wording and lists the decisions behind the notice.

## What the legislation says about telling callers

Regulation 3 of the 2000 Regulations authorises a business to record communications on its own phone system for certain purposes, including establishing the existence of facts and ascertaining or demonstrating the standards that are achieved by people using the system in the course of their duties. As we read it, one of the conditions in regulation 3(2) is that the system controller "has made all reasonable efforts to inform every person who may use the telecommunication system in question that communications transmitted by means thereof may be intercepted."

In plain terms, as we read it: a business that records calls for those purposes is expected to make reasonable efforts to tell the people on the call. A short spoken notice at the start of the call is a common way to do that. This is our reading, not advice, and the Regulations contain further conditions that we do not cover here.

## What the ICO says

On proportionality, the ICO says it is not usually proportionate to monitor or record the content of calls in all cases, and that businesses could monitor calls where it is necessary to provide evidence of business transactions, or for training or quality control purposes. It also suggests considering whether itemised call records (who called whom, and when) are enough, rather than recording content.

On informing people, the ICO's own example describes a customer service call centre where customers "are informed during calls and are signposted to detailed privacy information". That is the pattern to copy: a short notice in the call, and the detail elsewhere.

## Example opening lines

These are examples to adapt, not approved legal wording.

- **When staff answer:** "Good morning, [hotel]. Calls are recorded for training and quality."
- **When an AI answers:** "Hello, you have reached [hotel]. I'm an AI assistant and this call is recorded. How can I help?"
- **Voicemail:** "You have reached [hotel]. Your message will be recorded so that we can reply."

Whatever the wording, keep it short, say it before the caller shares personal details, and make sure it matches what actually happens. If you say "for training and quality" but also use recordings for something else, the notice and the practice no longer match.

## Telling callers more than the opening line

A spoken sentence cannot carry everything. Put the rest in a privacy notice on your website and say where it is, for example in your booking confirmation or at the foot of your contact page. It should cover:

- Why you record, and the basis you rely on.
- Who can listen to or read recordings and transcripts.
- Where recordings and transcripts are stored.
- How long you keep them, and how a caller can ask about their data.

## If an AI answers the call

Two things should be said, and both fit in the same opening line: that the caller is speaking to an AI, and that the call is recorded. Leaving either out risks the caller feeling misled, which is bad for trust in the hotel. For the wider legal picture, see [Is it legal to use an AI receptionist at a UK hotel?](https://gethanas.com/blog/is-it-legal-to-use-an-ai-receptionist-at-a-uk-hotel)

An AI vendor will usually be your data processor, acting on your instructions, so ask for its terms. Check where audio and transcripts go, as speech providers are often outside the UK.

## Four decisions behind the notice

Before you write the line, decide:

1. **Why you record.** If you cannot name a purpose, consider not recording content.
2. **What you say.** One sentence, at the start, matching the purpose.
3. **Where it is kept.** Which system, which country, who has access.
4. **How long.** A fixed retention period, and a way to delete on request.

## A short checklist

1. Purpose written down.
2. Opening line recorded or scripted on every phone route, including out-of-hours (see our [out-of-hours call handling checklist](https://gethanas.com/blog/out-of-hours-call-handling-checklist-for-small-hotels)).
3. Privacy notice on your website covering recordings and transcripts.
4. Staff told that calls are recorded and why.
5. Retention period set and enforced.
6. Vendor terms and data locations checked.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do UK hotels have to tell callers that calls are recorded?

As we read regulation 3 of the 2000 Regulations, a business relying on it for recording is expected to make all reasonable efforts to inform people that calls may be intercepted. Telling callers at the start of the call is the usual approach. Ask a solicitor about your own case.

### Is "calls may be recorded" enough?

It is a common phrase, and the ICO example describes informing customers during calls and signposting detailed privacy information. A clear sentence plus a privacy notice is a sensible pairing. We do not say what is legally sufficient.

### Can I record calls for any reason?

The ICO says it is not usually proportionate to record content in all cases, and gives evidence of business transactions, training and quality control as reasons a business could record. Have a stated purpose.

### Do I need to say it again if the call is transferred?

We found nothing on this in the sources we read. Say it on whichever line the caller reaches first, and take advice if your set-up transfers calls between lines.

### Does an AI receptionist need to say the call is recorded?

If it records or transcribes calls, the same expectation applies. Hanas, by its own site, says it at the start of every call.
